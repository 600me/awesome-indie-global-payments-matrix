The trap: a formation fee is not a holding cost

Every comparison post on the internet is built on the same three numbers. Firstbase charges $399 for a company formation package. Stripe Atlas charges $500. Doola's Starter plan is $297. Sort ascending and Doola looks like the cheapest way to own a US company, Firstbase looks mid-market, and Stripe Atlas looks like the premium option you buy when you already have investors.

That sort is useless, and the reason is arithmetic, not opinion. A US company has three recurring obligations that are completely independent of the person who filed the paperwork: the state wants an annual report or franchise tax, the state wants a registered agent physically present in the state, and the IRS wants a Form 5472 attached to a pro forma Form 1120 if the owner is a non-resident alien. Only the first of those is a one-time event, and the cheapest headline fee can select the most expensive state.

Here is the asymmetry in one sentence: Wyoming charges $60 a year and has no state income tax; Delaware charges $300 a year for an LLC and $450 or more for a C-Corp. Choose the state, and you have chosen two-thirds of your three-year bill, no matter whose checkout page you used.

The founder who compares formation fees alone therefore makes a decision about year one while signing up for years two and three. A $101 cheaper formation fee is worth nothing if it locks in $240 a year of extra state cost. That is exactly what the Delaware-only rule at Stripe Atlas does.

What each provider actually sells

Before the tables, the product shapes have to be on the table, because they are not comparable line items.

  • Firstbase, $399 one-time formation. A formation package priced once, not a subscription. You choose the entity type and — crucially — the state. Wyoming LLC is available. Registered agent service is an optional add-on at roughly $99 a year, and tax filing is quoted separately rather than bundled. The structure is modular: you pay for what the company actually needs this year.
  • Stripe Atlas, $500 one-time formation. A tightly engineered pipeline that produces a Delaware entity, an EIN, and a Stripe account that works. The first year of registered agent is included; renewals land at roughly $100 a year from year two. The state is Delaware. There is no Wyoming option, and the package is designed around companies that intend to raise venture capital and issue equity.
  • Doola, $297 a year Starter, or $1,999 a year Total Compliance. A subscription, not a one-time fee. Starter covers the formation workflow and ongoing registered agent and address service; Total Compliance bundles the tax and bookkeeping work, including the federal filings a foreign-owned LLC needs. The $297 is an annual number, and the state fee is added on top in both plans.

That difference in shape — one-time versus annual — is where the marketing comparison dies. Three years of $297 is $891 before a single state fee. Three years of $1,999 is $5,997. Neither number appears next to the $399 and $500 in a "formation cost" column.

Why Stripe Atlas cannot give you Wyoming

The Delaware-only rule is not laziness. Stripe Atlas exists to produce a company that can open a Stripe account, issue equity and pass an investor's diligence checklist, and Delaware is the jurisdiction that ecosystem is built on. Investors' standard documents assume Delaware corporate law, the Delaware Court of Chancery produces predictable precedent, and a C-Corp's stock plan, 83(b) elections and preferred-share mechanics all have well-worn templates there. If you intend to raise a priced round from US funds, paying $300 to $450 a year for that predictability is not waste. It is the cost of the asset you are selling.

The problem is that most non-US founders buying a formation package are not raising a US round. They are an indie developer or a two-person SaaS team who needs a US entity for one reason: to accept card payments as a US business, hold a US business bank account and stop paying a Merchant of Record 5%. That founder is paying a Delaware franchise tax for a legal infrastructure they will never use.

The decision rule: choose Delaware when a priced equity round, a US stock plan or a US acquirer is on the roadmap within 24 months. Choose Wyoming when you are optimising for a low, predictable annual bill and privacy — Wyoming does not publish member names in its public filings. Do not choose Delaware because a formation page defaulted to it.

There is a second-order cost that the fee tables never show: converting later. A Wyoming LLC can convert into a Delaware C-Corp when a round actually happens, and the conversion is a normal, well-documented exercise. Paying for Delaware for three years in case a round materialises in year four is paying insurance against a decision you can simply make later.

IRS Form 5472 and pro forma Form 1120: the $25,000 line

This is the part of the cost model that decides whether any of the above is affordable, and it is identical for all three providers. A single-member LLC owned by a non-resident alien is a disregarded entity for income tax, but it is still a reporting corporation. It must file IRS Form 5472 together with a pro forma Form 1120, disclosing reportable transactions between the foreign owner and the US company — including contributions, distributions, and amounts paid on the owner's behalf.

The penalty for failing to file, or for filing an incomplete form, is $25,000 per form, per year. It is not a percentage of revenue and it is not waived because the company had no profit or no activity. Additional penalties can accrue for continuing failures after IRS notice. This single line item dwarfs the entire three-year difference between every provider in the table above, which is why "cheap formation" is a meaningless claim if the filing is not handled.

Three things are worth stating precisely, because they are the usual sources of a five-figure surprise:

  • No activity is not an exemption. A dormant LLC with a foreign owner still has a filing obligation. Zero revenue generates a zero-information return, not a skipped one.
  • The form is attached to a pro forma 1120. Sending a 5472 alone is an incomplete filing. The two documents travel together.
  • Reasonable cause is a defence, not a plan. The IRS can abate the penalty where the failure was due to reasonable cause and the taxpayer acted in good faith, but that is an argument made after a notice arrives, with professional fees attached, and it is not something to budget around.

What the filing actually costs

There are three legitimate routes, and their price differences explain most of the spread in the market:

  • Do it yourself: $0 in fees, several hours of learning, and the full $25,000 exposure if you get the transaction categories wrong. Suitable if you already file US returns and enjoy this work.
  • A specialist preparer or CPA: typically a few hundred dollars a year for a straightforward single-member LLC with a handful of owner transactions. This is the route most first-time foreign founders should price.
  • A bundled compliance subscription: Doola's Total Compliance at $1,999 a year covers the tax preparation and bookkeeping alongside the agent and address. It is the most expensive route per year and the one with the fewest moving parts.

Three founder profiles, three correct answers

The solo SaaS founder with no funding plans. $8,000 MRR, one product, customers in forty countries, no intention of raising. Wyoming LLC, modular agent, independent 5472 preparer. Three-year basket $2,076, or $692 a year. This is the configuration the $876 row exists for, and it is the one that makes the US LLC route obviously cheaper than a 5% Merchant of Record.

The founder who will raise in 18 months. Delaware C-Corp, standard equity documents, the full Atlas pipeline. Three-year basket $3,250 with a preparer, plus the corporate and franchise filings a C-Corp adds. The extra $300 to $450 a year is the entry ticket to the financing market, and it is cheap relative to a botched conversion.

The founder who wants one invoice and no admin. Doola Total Compliance at $1,999 a year, or Starter at $297 if the tax work is handled elsewhere. You are buying the removal of a monthly admin task, not the lowest total. That is a legitimate purchase, as long as you know you are paying roughly $5,000 over three years for it compared with the modular route.

The payback, in revenue terms

The whole entity decision only makes sense against the processing bill it is meant to reduce. At $8,000 MRR with a $40 average order value, a 5% Merchant of Record costs 5% plus about 50 cents per order: $500 a month, which is $6,000 a year. Stripe's US card rate plus the 1.5% international surcharge on a 70% international card mix charges $376 a month in processing fees on the same volume.

The structure sits on top of that. Spread the three-year totals over three years and add the $400 preparer allowance, and the annual holding cost of each route is: Firstbase Wyoming $692, Stripe Atlas Delaware LLC $933, Doola Total Compliance $2,059.
